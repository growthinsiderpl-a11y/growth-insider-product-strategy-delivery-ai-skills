# Stakeholder, Board, and Product Communication

Use this hub for decision narratives, stakeholder alignment, board-ready product updates, and communication artifacts that preserve uncertainty without losing clarity.

## Purpose

This hub helps product teams explain what changed, what decision is needed, what evidence supports the recommendation, and what uncertainty must remain visible. It is for internal product communication, not for public messaging.

## What This Hub Owns

- Board and executive narrative design.
- Stakeholder update framing.
- Decision memos and trade-off explanation.
- Communication around roadmap changes.
- Product storylines tied to evidence and constraints.
- Meeting follow-through on product decisions.

## Route Away When

- The artifact is public relations or external campaign communication.
- The real need is detailed delivery scheduling better handled in operations.
- The narrative would make claims unsupported by product evidence.
- The work is motivational theater rather than a real product update.
- The request belongs to market messaging owned by marketing.

## Core Questions

- Who needs to decide, endorse, or align?
- What changed since the last communication?
- Which uncertainty should stay visible?
- Which trade-off is likely to be challenged?
- What is the concise ask from this communication?
- How do we avoid overpromising while still being clear?

## Evidence To Gather

- Current performance and product learning.
- Decision alternatives considered.
- Delivery, market, or governance constraints.
- Customer evidence that supports the narrative.
- Risk and dependency notes.
- The next review date and the signal to revisit.

## Decision Procedure

### Define the Audience

- Choose one primary audience per artifact.
- Translate technical, research, or metric detail into decision language.
- State what action the audience should take.
- Match depth to stakes and available meeting time.
- Note what context the audience already has and what it does not.

### Build the Narrative

- Start with context and the decision or ask.
- Show evidence before recommendation.
- Include trade-offs and what is not being done.
- Note risks and mitigations plainly.
- Show what changed since the prior communication.

### Handle Tension

- Anticipate objections from finance, sales, support, or delivery partners.
- Prepare responses rooted in evidence and constraints.
- Avoid defensive overstatement.
- State what would change the recommendation.
- Treat disagreement as an input to the decision, not as a narrative problem to erase.

### Close Cleanly

- State next steps, owners, and dates.
- Distinguish commitments from hypotheses and open questions.
- Capture decisions made in the meeting.
- Reuse the narrative only if the assumptions still hold.
- Archive the result where future product planning can reference it.

## Failure Modes

- Presenting optimistic product theater instead of a decision narrative.
- Burying risks to avoid friction.
- Copying the roadmap into a board update without a real ask.
- Using jargon that obscures the trade-off.
- Failing to state the ask.
- Turning uncertainty into vagueness.

## Cross-Skill Handoffs

- Marketing owns external announcements, campaign copy, and market-facing language.
- Engineering owns deep technical release notes where those are required.
- Product owns internal decision narrative, roadmap explanation, and board framing.
- Governance can codify recurring update patterns without taking over product judgment.

## Expected Outputs

- Board update narrative.
- Executive memo.
- Stakeholder alignment note.
- Decision FAQ.
- Meeting follow-through summary.
- Roadmap-change explanation note.

## Example Prompts

- Prepare a board update about our delayed roadmap and the decision it creates.
- Rewrite our stakeholder note so evidence and uncertainty are both visible.
- Turn a discovery finding into an executive decision memo.

## Decision Rules

- If a board update cannot state the ask in one sentence, the story is still too loose.
- The strongest stakeholder memo names both what changed and why it changed.
- Product communication should not flatten uncertainty into false confidence.
- One chart is enough when it genuinely explains the decision.
- A roadmap delay narrative becomes credible when it ties the delay to stronger risk control or stronger learning.
- If disagreement is predictable, the memo should surface it directly rather than hoping tone will hide it.

## Worked Example

- A board asks why a promised premium launch slipped by one quarter.
- A weak update says engineering needed more time and the team remained aligned.
- A stronger update says the team narrowed scope after evidence showed the governed segment valued auditability more than breadth, and the release should be staged to protect trust.
- That narrative gives the board a real decision frame, not a status excuse.
- It also sets up the next review by naming the activation, retention, or trust signal that will determine expansion.

## What Stakeholders Need To Hear Clearly

- What changed since the last plan and what evidence caused the change.
- What decision or endorsement is being requested now.
- What the team is not doing so the audience can understand the trade-off.
- What signal or milestone will justify the next update or decision.
