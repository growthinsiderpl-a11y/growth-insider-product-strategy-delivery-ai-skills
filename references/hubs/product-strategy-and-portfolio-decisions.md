# Product Strategy and Portfolio Decisions

Use this hub to turn broad strategy pressure into explicit product choices, portfolio boundaries, investment logic, and reviewable bets.

## Purpose

This hub helps product leaders decide where the product should compete, which customer problems deserve scarce capacity, which bets should be expanded or retired, and how the portfolio should be explained to leadership without pretending certainty that does not exist.

## What This Hub Owns

- Portfolio themes and allocation logic.
- Segment and problem selection.
- Strategic fit and explicit non-goals.
- Bet sizing under constrained capacity.
- Reviewable statements of why now, why this, and why not the alternatives.
- Links between strategy claims and measurable product outcomes.

## Route Away When

- The real need is performance marketing, campaign planning, or channel mix.
- The core question is solution architecture or implementation topology.
- The request is creative production with no product decision attached.
- The work is generic mission language with no strategic choice to make.
- The analysis is purely financial and does not change a product decision.

## Core Questions

- What strategic choice is actually being made this quarter or half year?
- Which customer problem deserves scarce capacity now?
- What evidence makes this bet stronger than adjacent bets?
- What revenue, retention, trust, or strategic risk appears if the team does nothing?
- Which portfolio item is being delayed, narrowed, or killed to fund this choice?
- What would invalidate the strategy within one review cycle?

## Evidence To Gather

- Revenue concentration by segment, product line, or use case.
- Retention and expansion patterns by segment.
- Customer complaint clusters tied to churn, stalled adoption, or sales friction.
- Delivery capacity limits that cap the number of simultaneous bets.
- Founder, board, compliance, or contractual constraints that narrow feasible options.
- Proof that the chosen bet aligns with the broader operating model and current business risk.

## Decision Procedure

### Frame the Decision

- Write the strategic decision as one sentence with an owner and decision date.
- Separate the desired business outcome from favored feature ideas.
- Name the constrained resource, such as engineering capacity, market attention, design bandwidth, or implementation risk.
- State the decision horizon in weeks, quarters, or planning windows.
- Describe what happens if no decision is made.
- Record what this strategy work explicitly does not cover.

### Define Portfolio Logic

- Group current work into run, improve, explore, and retire.
- Describe the intended contribution of each bucket to revenue, retention, trust, or risk reduction.
- Identify overlaps where two initiatives compete for the same customer pain or the same internal capacity.
- Make non-goals explicit so scope creep cannot masquerade as strategic flexibility.
- Treat portfolio balance as an active choice rather than a default status quo.
- Call out any initiative that survives mainly because nobody wants to retire it.

### Compare Strategic Bets

- Use explicit criteria such as customer value, strategic fit, evidence quality, reversibility, time to learning, and dependency load.
- Write the rationale next to each criterion so reviewers can challenge the reasoning directly.
- Show which option wins under current constraints and why.
- State the condition under which another option would become preferable.
- Keep comparisons traceable to evidence, not only to stakeholder preference.
- Highlight the assumptions most likely to flip the recommendation.

### Commit and Review

- Translate the recommendation into one portfolio move, such as fund, narrow, defer, or retire.
- Name the work that will stop, shrink, or move later as a result.
- Assign a review window and a falsification trigger.
- Define leading signals before lagging business outcomes move.
- Prepare a concise stakeholder and board narrative that preserves both the choice and the uncertainty.
- Store the decision rationale so later roadmap edits can be judged against the original logic.

## Failure Modes

- Turning portfolio review into a list of project status updates.
- Equating loud stakeholder demand with strategic importance.
- Funding discovery or delivery work without a real decision owner.
- Keeping zombie roadmap themes because no one wants to kill them.
- Pretending a heuristic ranking is objective truth.
- Failing to name what will not be funded.

## Cross-Skill Handoffs

- Send acquisition channel choices, launch campaigns, and message testing to marketing.
- Send system topology, deep architecture, and repository planning to engineering.
- Keep the product-side trade-off, portfolio choice, outcome logic, and evidence boundary here.
- Share the decision rationale with finance and leadership without outsourcing product judgment to them.

## Expected Outputs

- Strategy memo with explicit trade-offs.
- Portfolio allocation view with rationale.
- Quarterly or half-year bet statement.
- Retire or defer list with business consequences.
- Review schedule with invalidation conditions.
- Stakeholder brief that explains the change in direction.

## Example Prompts

- Choose between self-serve expansion and governed enterprise workflow depth.
- We have three product lines and one core team. Which product should receive the next quarter of focus?
- Rewrite our annual plan as explicit product choices rather than an aspiration list.

## Decision Rules

- If a portfolio theme has no measurable consequence of delay, it is probably not a real priority.
- A bet that only wins when several optimistic assumptions all hold should be treated as an explore bet, not a committed bet.
- When two bets target the same customer pain, prefer the one with stronger evidence, cleaner reversibility, or faster learning under current constraints.
- Do not label work strategic if the only argument is that leadership cares about it.
- Retiring a stale initiative is often more valuable than adding another underfunded initiative beside it.
- If the board needs a new strategy story every month, the portfolio is probably changing faster than evidence can justify.

## Worked Example

- A collaboration platform can fund either AI-generated summaries or enterprise-grade permissions cleanup.
- Support evidence shows enterprise admins are delaying expansion because permission behavior feels risky and hard to audit.
- The AI summaries idea demos well, but its evidence is mostly excitement rather than repeatable behavior.
- The permissions cleanup bet improves trust, reduces expansion friction, and strengthens fit in the segment already showing durable value.
- In that case, the strategy artifact should explicitly fund trust and governance depth while recording what evidence would be needed before the AI summaries bet is promoted.

## What To Put In The Memo

- Name the chosen bet, the rejected alternatives, and the cost of leaving them unfunded for one review cycle.
- Show which metric should move first if the bet is working and which signal would make the team stop defending it.
- State whether the decision deepens the product in a winning segment or opens risk in a new one.
- Record the capacity, dependency, or political constraint that shaped the decision so future reviewers do not invent a cleaner history.
